Piriformis Syndrome vs. True Lumbar Sciatica: Pinpointing Deep Pelvic Nerve Entrapment

Over 40% of patients diagnosed with 'sciatica' do not have a spine problem at all—their sciatic nerve is being strangled by the pear-shaped piriformis muscle deep inside the buttock. Here is how to tell the difference and get lasting relief.

Dr. M. Mehraj Abdullah, M.D (Acu) ATAMA Reg. No: 20261714 / TN • November 06, 2026 • 6 min read
Accurate differential diagnosis separates disc herniations from pelvic piriformis entrapment.

The Anatomical Trap Beneath the Gluteus Maximus

The sciatic nerve passes directly underneath—and in 15% of the population, straight through—the belly of the piriformis muscle. Prolonged sitting on hard chairs, driving, or pelvic torsion causes this muscle to become hypertonic, suffocating the nerve and mimicking lumbar disc herniation.

Differential Diagnosis via Plantar Reflex Sensitivity

At Balance & Bliss, Dr. Mehraj differentiates the two conditions by palpating the pelvic and hip reflex zones located on the lateral aspect of the heel. Intense tenderness on the outer heel margin combined with a clear spinal reflex indicates primary piriformis entrapment.

Doctor's Rule: Look Beyond the Spine

If MRI scans of your lower back show only mild bulges that don't match your pain, investigate the piriformis muscle. Releasing pelvic tension restores pain-free sitting immediately.

Targeted Reflex Decompression of the Deep Rotators

Applying sustained pressure to the hip and sciatic reflex zones on both the dorsal and plantar margins triggers an immediate neuromuscular release of the deep hip external rotators, freeing the sciatic nerve from pelvic strangulation.
